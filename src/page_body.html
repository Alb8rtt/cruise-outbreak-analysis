CDC Vessel Sanitation Program · 1993–2026

Cruise passengers fall ill at 3.8 times the crew rate on the same ship

Crew live aboard for months. Passengers are there for a week. They drink the same water, eat from the same galley and touch the same handrails. One group gets sick far more often, and the gap holds in every subgroup this dataset is large enough to test.

Analysis by Albert Rafael Alonso · 425 investigations, — vessels · Source: CDC Vessel Sanitation Program

The gap survives every cut tested here

Pooled attack rate for passengers and crew, inside each subgroup the data supports. Every occupancy band, every era that has paired counts, both seasons. The ratio ranges from 2.7x to 4.6x and never approaches parity. Cruise line is not among the cuts: most operators appear only a handful of times and 10 appear exactly once. Operator was not tested at all, so this is a claim about the cuts below, not about every cut available.

Passengers Crew

Paired on the voyage, so ship, route, water supply and galley are held constant. Wilcoxon signed-rank p = 1.2 × 10−47, rank-biserial r = 0.96, median per-voyage difference 4.11 percentage points. This is the finding the project rests on.

Persistent across eras, but no significant trend either way

Pooled rate per era. The crew rate fell to a low in 2016–2020 and rebounded. Neither series shows a statistically detectable trend in either direction.

Passengers Crew

Neither trend is statistically significant across individual voyages: crew ρ = −0.097, p = 0.092; passengers ρ = +0.011, p = 0.850. An earlier version of this analysis reported the crew trend as significant. That figure came from a sample cut at 2020, which excluded the post-2020 rebound, and the cut was what produced the significance. It has been withdrawn and replaced with the full-sample figures above.

Most "repeat outbreaks" are one episode counted twice

47 outbreaks began within thirty days of a previous one on the same vessel. 38 of them began within two days of the previous voyage ending and one more overlapped it outright, with a median gap of zero days. The most economical reading is that these are the following sailing of the same itinerary, though the dataset carries no itinerary field to confirm it.

The economical explanation is a single outbreak spanning a turnaround, logged as two investigations. That matters for anyone using VSP records to count outbreak frequency, because the counts overstate the number of distinct episodes. I originally read this as evidence that ships return to service too early. That conclusion is withdrawn.

Clustering is real, but smaller than the obvious test suggests

How many repeats you would expect if outbreaks landed on ships at random depends entirely on what you let "at random" mean. Shuffling ship names across the whole 1993–2026 range lets a 1997 vessel take a 2020 date.

Observed Expected, shuffled within calendar year

All three nulls stay significant at p < 0.0005 across 2,000 permutations, but the effect size shrinks as the null gets fairer. At the 90-day window it falls from 3.4x under an unrestricted shuffle to 1.4x when the shuffle is restricted to within a calendar year. The chart shows that most conservative comparison. The 90-day figures are the ones to quote, because the independence assumption the test needs is weakest at 30 days, where most repeats are consecutive sailings of the same loop.

More passengers aboard does not mean a worse outbreak

Every outbreak with published counts, by how many passengers were aboard. The intuitive theory is that crowded megaships breed worse outbreaks. It is not supported.

Spearman ρ = −0.061, p = 0.292 across 303 outbreaks. The least-squares line slopes gently downward, but that slope is carried by a handful of very small expedition ships with extreme rates and it does not survive a rank test. Voyage length does not predict severity either (ρ = +0.020, p = 0.726). Both null results are published rather than dropped.

When outbreaks happen

Investigated outbreaks by the month the voyage began, with the attack rate computed separately over the smaller set of voyages that carry published counts.

The two views use different populations, which is why they are on separate toggles rather than the same axis. Outbreaks concentrate in winter, but so does Caribbean sailing volume and occupancy, and this dataset has no voyage counts by month. The seasonal pattern cannot be separated from seasonal exposure, so no inference is drawn from it here.

What this does and does not establish

Established

Passengers fall ill at roughly four times the crew rate on the same voyages, in every subgroup large enough to test, with a very large effect size. That is the one result here that survives the paired signed-rank test and all eleven subgroup cuts.

Not established

Any trend over time, in either direction. Across individual voyages the passenger rate gives ρ = +0.011, p = 0.850, which is not evidence of stability but an absence of evidence either way. Also not established: why the gap exists. This dataset carries no protocol, immunity or demographic variable. Protocol differences, acquired immunity from months of exposure, a younger crew population, and crew under-reporting are all live explanations, and nothing here ranks them.

Withdrawn

That crew outcomes improved while passenger outcomes did not, which rested on a truncated sample. And that vessels return to service too early, which rested on repeat outbreaks that turn out to be consecutive sailings.

The data

Method and limitations

  • 428 investigations were transcribed; 425 are analysed. Three carry month-only sailing dates in the CDC source and cannot be placed on a timeline. Nothing was estimated or imputed.
  • Rates come from the 303 records carrying both passenger and crew counts, spanning 2004 to 2024. CDC publishes counts only for closed reporting years. The longer 1993–2026 span applies to outbreak frequency only.
  • Crew under-reporting is one of four live explanations for the gap, alongside protocol, acquired immunity and age mix. Crew have a financial incentive not to report illness that takes them off paid duty. This data cannot separate them.
  • CDC investigates once reported illness reaches 3% of passengers or crew, so every rate here is conditional on an outbreak being investigated. This is not a rate across all cruises.
  • Outbreak counts by cruise line track fleet size and voyage volume. They are not a safety ranking.
  • Thirteen records carry a CDC percentage that disagrees with CDC's own published counts, seven on the passenger figure and six on the crew figure, and four carry unusable voyage lengths. All are flagged in the dataset rather than silently corrected.
  • Records for 2019–2022 came from a public mirror of the CDC page because the CDC archive URL was unreachable at collection time. All source pages were read by model-mediated text extraction rather than direct HTML parsing.

Source

CDC Vessel Sanitation Program outbreak investigations. VSP has jurisdiction over every cruise ship carrying 13 or more passengers that calls on a US port.
